How ‘Professionalism’ Silences the Neurodivergent

Part III: Recovery & Rebuilding · By Jonathan Franko
Series Note: Essay 17 examined what corporate metric systems measure and what they choose not to. This essay applies the same lens to a softer, harder-to-quantify metric — professionalism — and argues that what it measures, in practice, is conformity to a particular style of social performance that has very little to do with competence. The people who pay the cost of the confusion are predictably distributed.

For four and a half years I worked as a Business Analyst at a Fortune 500 paint and coatings company, leading automation projects, building reporting systems, and producing the kind of documented, measurable results performance reviews are designed to surface. The reviews surfaced them. They also surfaced something else, year after year, in the section reserved for development feedback. The feedback was about communication. It was about tone. It was about how my directness landed. It was rarely, if ever, about whether the analysis was correct, the project delivered, the system functional, or the relationship with the partner team productive. Those things were generally not in dispute. The development area was always the same: the way I said the things I said.

This is a common pattern in the careers of neurodivergent professionals, and the pattern is the subject of this essay. The work is sound. The deliverables ship. The relationships function. The feedback, year after year, focuses on a register of communication that is harder to define than it sounds: more diplomatic, less direct, more comfortable, less blunt. The vocabulary varies by industry and manager, but the underlying instruction is consistent. The instruction is to perform a kind of social fluency that is invisible to the people for whom it is native and exhausting to the people for whom it is not. The performance is called professionalism, and the failure to perform it is treated as a deficiency in the worker rather than a feature of the evaluation system. That confusion — between competence and performance — is what the essay is about.

I want to be careful about a few things before I begin. I am not arguing that all communication norms are arbitrary. They are not. Some communication choices genuinely improve outcomes, and some communication failures genuinely harm them. Nor am I arguing that neurodivergent workers should be exempt from feedback on how they communicate. They should not. What I am arguing is that a great deal of what gets labeled professionalism in performance reviews, hiring decisions, and informal social judgments is not communication quality at all. It is communication style — a particular set of tonal, postural, and rhythmic conventions that map onto a particular neurotype — and that the conflation of style with quality is producing predictable outcomes for the people whose neurotype does not happen to be the one the conventions were built around.

What Professionalism Measures, in Practice

Strip away the abstractions and the operational definition of professionalism in most corporate environments is fairly concrete. It includes sustained eye contact during conversation, tone modulation that signals interest or deference at appropriate intervals, the reading of social cues that indicate when to speak and when to defer, body language that telegraphs engagement, and the packaging of ideas in forms that prioritize the comfort of the listener. It also includes a particular cadence of email and message: the softening preambles, the receipt-style acknowledgments, the hedges that signal that the writer is not insisting too forcefully on the position the writer is, in fact, taking. None of these are bad in themselves. They are forms of social lubrication, and social lubrication has its uses. The problem is that they are treated, increasingly, as proxies for competence in environments where they have no causal relationship to the underlying work.

The clinical psychologist and educator Devon Price has written at length about this conflation in the context of autism and ADHD. Price’s argument is that neurodivergent people are required, from an early age, to suppress their natural styles of communication and adopt neurotypical performances in order to access education, employment, and social belonging. The performance is called masking. Masking is not a metaphor. It is the active, conscious, exhausting work of monitoring one’s own tone, expression, gesture, and rhythm against an internalized template of what a neurotypical person would do in the same situation. It is a labor performed continuously, in the background, on top of whatever cognitive work the person is actually being paid for. The labor is invisible to the people for whom the template is native. It is constantly visible to the person performing it.

The cost of masking is not abstract. Energy spent monitoring one’s expression is energy unavailable for analysis. Attention spent calibrating tone is attention unavailable for the substance of what is being said. The cognitive resources that go into producing a comfortable interaction are taken from the same finite pool that the work itself draws on. A neurodivergent professional who is masking effectively in a meeting is, by the end of the meeting, operating at a fraction of the cognitive capacity their colleagues are operating at, because their colleagues have not been doing the second job — the performance job — that the meeting demanded of them. The output the company observes is the output of someone working under load it cannot see. The reviews that reward effective masking are rewarding the depletion of the resource the company is also asking the person to spend on the work.

The Lose-Lose of the Mask

The structural problem is that the mask, however effective, is not stable. If the masking succeeds, the worker is read as professional but is operating below their actual capacity and is steadily exhausting themselves. If the masking fails — if the eye contact slips, if the tone reads as too direct, if the hedge gets dropped — the worker is read as awkward, unpolished, or unprofessional regardless of the quality of the work being produced. The work is held to one standard. The person doing it is held to another. The two standards are inconsistent in a way the evaluation system is not designed to surface. The reviews that result describe a person whose work is solid but whose communication is somehow off, and the gap between those two observations is the place the mask is leaking. The system reads the gap as a deficiency. The person reads the gap as the cost of the labor the system is not paying them for.

The lose-lose is the part of the dynamic that is hardest to communicate to neurotypical managers, because it requires the manager to hold two facts in mind at once. The first fact is that the worker is producing good results. The second fact is that the worker is producing those results while running a parallel performance the manager is not seeing. Most managers can hold the first fact. Most managers struggle to hold the second, because the second is invisible by design. The mask is functioning as intended. The cost is being paid in a register the performance review has no column for. The manager, looking at the review, sees a competent worker with a communication issue, and the recommended development plan is, predictably, to work on the communication. The development plan, in other words, is to mask harder. The recommendation is to spend more of the finite cognitive pool on the performance the system rewards, which leaves less of it for the work the system is also paying for, which over time produces the burnout the system then attributes to the worker’s personal resilience.

There is a particular flavor of feedback that this dynamic produces, and anyone who has been on the receiving end of it will recognize it. The feedback acknowledges that the work is good. It then notes that the delivery could be more diplomatic. It suggests workshops on communication, or executive presence, or stakeholder management. It does not, in general, suggest that the evaluator should examine their own response to direct speech, or that the workplace culture might be selecting for a particular cognitive style rather than for the quality of contribution. The asymmetry is the point. The burden of adaptation is placed entirely on the worker whose neurotype is in the minority, and the adaptation is treated as a personal development opportunity rather than as an accommodation the workplace is, in effect, requiring without naming it.

Hiring Reproduces the Pattern

The same dynamic that produces the development feedback also produces the hiring decision. Most corporate interview processes are, in practice, evaluations of social performance. The candidate is judged on the firmness of their handshake, the steadiness of their eye contact, the smoothness of their conversational transitions, the warmth of their small talk, the polish of their narrative responses to behavioral questions. The work history is a backdrop. The performance is the foreground. A brilliant but awkward candidate with a strong portfolio loses, regularly and predictably, to a competent but polished candidate with a weaker portfolio, because the interview is structured to surface the polish and to treat the polish as the signal. The hiring manager exits the interview with a strong impression of one candidate and a weaker impression of the other, and the strong impression is, when one looks at it closely, an impression of having been comfortable for forty-five minutes.

The closest I came to seeing this play out at PPG, I saw only at a remove, through the grapevine, after I had already been let go. A senior developer on the team departed the company some months after my own elimination. The manager who had decided my role would be eliminated was the same manager who decided how to fill the vacancy, and the decision was to hire externally rather than promote from the bench of developers who had been building their careers inside the team for the entire span of my tenure there. I do not know the deliberations that led to the choice, and I do not want to overstate what I do not know. I know two things. The first is structural: an external hire is, by construction, selected almost entirely on the interview — the forty-five minutes of polish — because the interview is nearly all the information the panel has, while the internal bench is passed over despite being the only candidates whose actual work the organization has watched for years. The second is the outcome: the external hire lasted about a year in the role. I do not know why, and I will not pretend to. But a process that passes over people with years of observable work in favor of a candidate known mostly through an interview, and produces a hire who is gone within a year, has measured something. Whatever it measured, it was not durability in the job.

The case is not unusual. It is the default outcome of an interview process that treats performance as a proxy for competence. The cost is distributed unevenly. The people who lose are the people whose performance does not match their competence, which is to say, disproportionately, the people whose neurotype produces a different style of presentation than the one the interview rewards. The people who win are the people whose performance is at least as good as their competence, which is a smaller and more specific population than the language of meritocracy suggests. The hiring process is selecting for a trait. It is calling the trait competence. The trait is, in fact, presentation. The two are not the same, and the gap between them is the place the inequity lives.

Educational Gatekeeping, in Miniature

There is a related dynamic at the level of education and credentialing, and I want to describe it because it has stayed with me. At no point during my four-and-a-half years at PPG did the company do anything that resembled support for further education outside of itself. I had asked, at various points, about advanced degree programs that aligned with the analytical work I was doing. The answer, however it was phrased, came down to the same answer: that was not the kind of investment the company was going to make in someone in my role. The framing was not personal. It was structural. The roles the company invested in were a specific set of roles. Mine was not on the list. The list of roles eligible for the investment was, in effect, the list of roles the company had already decided were worth becoming more credentialed in. The list of roles not on it — mine among them — were the roles whose incumbents were treated as adequate at the credential level they already held, which meant, in practice, that any further credentialing the incumbents wanted to pursue would have to be pursued on their own time and at their own cost.

One conversation from this period stayed with me. I had mentioned, to a more senior developer, that I was looking into a certificate program at Carnegie Mellon. His response was a remark about how prestigious CMU was, delivered with a kind of measured astonishment that I was even looking into it. The remark was friendly on its surface. The subtext was that I was overreaching, and that the institution was not really, in his quiet assumption, the kind of institution someone in my role should be looking at. The remark was not personal in the way the speaker meant it. It was structural, and the speaker was a transmitter of a signal he had absorbed from environments that had been transmitting it to him for years. It is the dynamic of who gets taken seriously, who gets invested in, whose potential is recognized, and whose potential is quietly capped by other people’s perceptions before it has a chance to develop. The remark surfaced, in a single sentence, an entire layer of corporate professionalism that resists analysis because it does not have a paper trail.

The actual education ended up happening only after my employment at PPG was terminated. The agency I had over what I studied came not from the company that had employed me but from the public assistance programs I was relying on during the unemployment period. And even there, the CMU certificate I had been looking into stayed off the table, because of the way public grant funding is earmarked — not all programs at all institutions are eligible, and the categories that determine eligibility are not always the categories the learner would prefer. The gatekeeping that had operated through my employer was replaced, after my employer was gone, by a gatekeeping that operated through the funding mechanism. The names of the gates changed. The fact of the gates did not. This is the layer of professionalism that resists analysis because it does not have a paper trail. It is in the room before the meeting starts. It is in the tone of the introduction. It is in the structure of the funding form, and in the categories the form admits. It is in who gets the speaking slot at the conference and who gets thanked for being so dependable. The people in whose favor the layer operates do not, in general, perceive it as a layer at all. They perceive it as the natural surface of how the workplace works. For the people on the other side of it, the layer is the workplace.

When Organizations Name the Problem and Reinscribe It

A subset of large companies have developed an internal apparatus for talking about neurodivergence. The apparatus typically takes the form of a Business Resource Group or Employee Resource Group, supported by an executive sponsor, with periodic programming aligned to awareness months and disability days. The intentions behind these groups are not the issue. The intentions are, on the whole, sincere. What is worth examining is what the programming actually produces, because it tends to produce a particular kind of contradiction the participants do not always see.

A company I have worked for has such a group. The group posted, for one of the relevant awareness days, a message that drew the distinction between awareness and acceptance. The distinction is a real one, and it is taken seriously inside the disability community. Awareness asks the majority to notice that the minority exists. Acceptance asks the majority to change in response to that noticing. Awareness places the cost of accommodation on the minority. Acceptance places at least part of the cost on the institution. The post named the distinction correctly and argued that the organization should be moving from the first to the second. It was a good post. I appreciated it.

Two weeks later, the same group circulated a piece of content from a popular neurodivergence-focused publication. The piece was a productivity-advice article aimed at ADHD readers. Its content was the familiar genre of personal-productivity advice: scheduling tips, mindfulness suggestions, habit redesign, self-monitoring routines. It was the textbook version of awareness content. The entire burden of adaptation was placed on the individual reader, framed as personal strategies for managing a personal challenge, with no implication that the environment producing the challenge might be a legitimate object of change. The post about awareness-versus-acceptance had explicitly identified this framing as the problem. The follow-up post performed the framing. The juxtaposition was not noticed by the people circulating the content, because the two posts were operating in different cognitive registers — one structural, one individual — and the structural register did not survive contact with the next item in the editorial calendar. The group named the right framework, then immediately undercut it with its own content. This is the characteristic failure mode of corporate disability programming. It is not that the institution is opposed to acceptance. It is that the institution does not know how to operationalize acceptance, so it reverts to awareness while continuing to use the vocabulary of acceptance. The vocabulary travels. The practice does not.

I commented on the post, with what I thought was a measured observation about the pattern. The comment was analytical. It was not angry. A colleague read it as angry. He told me so, in a follow-up conversation, in tones that suggested he found the response disproportionate. The exchange is worth describing because it is a textbook instance of a dynamic neurodivergent workers will recognize. A structural observation, written in plain language, was read as a personal grievance. The content of the comment was not the source of the reading. The source of the reading was the absence, in the reader, of the frame the comment was operating inside. Without the frame, the comment had no obvious referent except the colleague’s relationship to me, and so he read it as being about him. It was not. It was about the apparatus the post was a small instance of. The reader supplied the missing context from the only context he had, which was the interpersonal one, and the interpersonal reading was that I was upset. The reading was projected back onto the comment as its meaning. The comment, once it was angry, had to be defused. The defusion took the form of a conversation about my tone.

This is the loop that keeps the apparatus stable. The structural observation, made in good faith, is read as a personal complaint. The personal complaint is then addressed at the personal level. The structural observation is never engaged at the structural level, because it was never received as a structural observation in the first place. The apparatus that produced the contradiction stays in place. The person who flagged the contradiction is treated as having had a reaction. The treatment of the reaction becomes the closure of the matter. Nothing has changed. The next awareness day will produce the same juxtaposition. The next observation will be received the same way. The system is consistent.

A Note on Privilege and Dismissal

There is one more dynamic to name, because it operates as the mirror image of the lose-lose described above and because leaving it unmentioned would falsify the picture. The same culture that penalizes neurodivergent workers for failing to mask is also, in certain settings, structured to dismiss their substantive contributions through a different vocabulary. The vocabulary is the language of privilege. The mechanism is straightforward. A neurodivergent worker who is not performing professionalism is read as awkward and is penalized accordingly. The same worker, when they shift register and offer substantive analysis, can be read as speaking from a position of unexamined advantage, and the analysis can be dismissed on that basis. The two readings are not consistent with each other — the worker is being penalized for failing to perform smoothness and then dismissed when they speak from any position other than smoothness — but the inconsistency is not visible to the system, because the two readings operate in separate domains. One is the performance domain. The other is the politics domain. The worker stands at the intersection and absorbs the costs of both.

I do not raise this to complain. I raise it because the complete picture of how professionalism functions as a silencing mechanism requires naming both faces of the silencing. The face that asks for smoothness and the face that dismisses the substance behind the smoothness are, in the cultures where both operate, parts of the same apparatus. The worker who tries to engage substantively is told their tone needs work. The worker whose tone is now perfect is told they are speaking from privilege. There is no register in which the substance can be heard. The function of professionalism, when it operates this way, is not to elevate communication. It is to ensure that the substance never has to be engaged with directly. The dismissals are different in idiom. They do the same job.

Toward a Workable Definition

None of the foregoing is an argument that professionalism, as a concept, should be abandoned. It is an argument that the working definition needs to be replaced with a more honest one. Professionalism, when it is doing useful work in an organization, is a set of behaviors that make the work better — clarity of communication, reliability of follow-through, integrity in dealing with colleagues and customers, restraint in personal conduct that would otherwise impose costs on others, and a basic regard for the dignity of the people one is working with. None of those behaviors require eye contact, tone modulation, smooth small talk, or the suppression of one’s natural cadence of speech. The behaviors that actually constitute professional excellence are accessible to people of any neurotype. The behaviors that have come to stand in for professionalism are accessible only to people of one.

The substitution — of style for substance, of performance for behavior, of comfort for clarity — is what produces the silencing. Replacing the working definition does not require new vocabulary. It requires the existing vocabulary to be applied to what it nominally describes. Clarity means that the listener can understand what the speaker is saying, not that the speaker has packaged it in a way that makes the listener comfortable with the conclusion. Reliability means that the work is done as agreed, not that the worker projects an air of dependability. Integrity means that the worker tells the truth, not that the worker delivers the truth in the register that makes the institution’s self-image easiest to maintain. The redefinition is simple. The implementation is not, because the existing definition is doing work the institution depends on. The work it is doing is the work of selecting for a particular kind of person, and a redefinition that did not select for that person would be a redefinition the institution would have to actively resist. The resistance is not always visible. It is built into the evaluation systems, the hiring rubrics, the casual remarks at the conference table, the body language of the room. The redefinition is a project of decades. It is worth starting.

A workable definition of professionalism would treat substance as the thing being measured, and would treat communication style as one of several methods by which substance can be conveyed. It would distinguish between communication that fails to convey substance and communication that conveys substance in a register the listener finds unfamiliar. The first is a problem. The second is not. It would also recognize that the cost of asking workers to perform a particular style is being paid by the workers, and that the payment is not free. The accommodation literature would call this the curb-cut effect: a change made for the benefit of one group tends to benefit others as well. A workplace that did not require the performance of professionalism would not only be more accessible to neurodivergent workers. It would be more accessible to everyone, because everyone, to varying degrees, is paying the cost of the performance. The neurodivergent worker is paying it most visibly. The neurotypical worker is paying it less visibly. The pool of cognitive resources being drained by the performance is a shared one. The institution treats it as free because it does not appear on the balance sheet. The workers know it is not free, because they are the balance sheet.

Closing

I have spent four and a half years at one employer and am now at another, in a similar industry, doing similar work, with the same brain and the same way of communicating. The reviews at the second employer will, in time, produce some version of the feedback the reviews at the first employer produced. The feedback will be about communication. It will be about tone. It will note that the work is sound and that the delivery could be more comfortable for the listener. I will receive the feedback with the same attention I always have. I will not, however, accept the premise that the feedback describes a deficiency in me. The premise is the part of the apparatus that needs to be examined. The feedback is the surface; the premise is the apparatus.

The work I do is the work the company hired me to do. The way I communicate is the way I communicate. The two are not in conflict. The conflict, when it exists, is between the way I communicate and the way the evaluation system expects communication to feel. That gap is real, and it has costs, and I have absorbed those costs for as long as I have been in white-collar work. I will continue to absorb some of them, because the alternative is to be unemployable, and unemployability is its own kind of cost. But I am no longer willing to absorb them in silence, or to participate in the polite fiction that the absorbing is a personal development project rather than the cost of a structural feature the institution has chosen not to redesign.

Competence does not require conformity. Integrity does not require pretense. The performance that gets called professionalism is, in many of the places where it is most insisted upon, neither of those things. It is a sorting mechanism dressed up as a virtue. The mechanism sorts predictably. The people on the receiving end of the sort have known this for as long as the sort has existed. The institutions doing the sorting are, as is the pattern with sorting institutions, the last to find out.

Maybe I was not unprofessional. Maybe I was just unmasked. And maybe that is what professionalism should mean.
