Why 'Resilience' Became the Corporate Coping Mechanism

Part II: The Human Cost Feb 19, 2026 · By Jonathan Franko
Series Note: This is the penultimate essay in Part II. Where Essays 8-13 examined specific mechanisms of harm, this essay examines the ideology that makes those mechanisms tolerable—the belief that the solution to institutional dysfunction is individual endurance.

The wellness email arrived during the layoffs. Not before them. Not as a preemptive acknowledgment that organizational upheaval might affect employee mental health. During them. While people were being pulled into meetings to learn their positions had been eliminated, the company's wellness program sent an email about mindfulness techniques. Breathing exercises. Suggested meditation apps. The organizational equivalent of handing someone an umbrella while you flood their house.

I don't think anyone sent that email with malicious intent. It was probably automated—scheduled weeks in advance as part of a wellness calendar that nobody thought to pause when the restructuring announcements began. But the timing captured something essential about how corporations approach the mental health of their employees: as a problem of individual regulation rather than institutional responsibility. The message wasn't "we recognize that what we're doing to you is harmful." It was "here are some tools to help you cope with what we're doing to you." The distinction matters. One requires the organization to change. The other requires the employee to adapt.

This essay is about that distinction—about how "resilience" became the corporate answer to institutional dysfunction, and why the language of bouncing back obscures the structural damage that shouldn't exist in the first place.

The Weaponization of Wellness

Corporate wellness programs have grown into a multi-billion-dollar industry, and the value proposition is simple: healthier employees cost less. Lower healthcare claims. Reduced absenteeism. Higher productivity. The business case for wellness is well-documented, and the programs it generates—gym subsidies, nutrition counseling, meditation apps, stress management workshops—are generally beneficial when they supplement a healthy work environment.

The problem arises when wellness programs substitute for a healthy work environment. When the organization's response to burnout is a meditation app rather than a manageable workload. When the response to anxiety is a breathing exercise rather than an examination of what's generating the anxiety. When the response to declining engagement is a wellness webinar rather than an honest conversation about why people are disengaging. In these cases, wellness programs don't address the problem. They absorb the complaint. They give the organization something to point to—"we offer wellness resources"—while the conditions creating the need for those resources remain unchanged.

The American Psychological Association has been clear on this point: individual coping strategies cannot fix institutional problems. Telling employees to meditate through a toxic feedback culture, breathe through an unreasonable workload, or journal through the anxiety of potential layoffs doesn't address the toxicity, the workload, or the uncertainty. It reframes institutional failures as individual challenges and then offers individual tools for challenges that are, by nature, collective. The effect is to shift responsibility from the organization that created the conditions to the employees enduring them.

At PPG, the wellness programming existed alongside the conditions I've described throughout this series. The feedback culture of Essay 10 operated simultaneously with wellness emails about stress management. The silence engineering of Essay 9 coexisted with town halls encouraging "open communication." The accommodation failures of Essay 12 ran parallel to an Abilities First network hosting awareness events. The wellness infrastructure and the dysfunction infrastructure occupied the same organization without apparent contradiction—because they served different functions. The wellness programs served the organization's external narrative (we care about employees). The dysfunction served the organization's operational priorities (maintain output, reduce cost, enforce compliance).

The result is what I'd call wellness theater: programming that performs institutional care without practicing it. The meditation app is real. The breathing exercise works. The stress management techniques are evidence-based. But offering them to employees whose stress is caused by the organization's own practices is like offering swimming lessons to someone you're holding underwater. The technique is sound. The context makes it absurd.

The financial incentive for wellness theater over genuine workplace improvement is straightforward. A meditation app subscription costs dollars per employee per month. Fixing a toxic feedback culture costs managerial change, leadership accountability, and the willingness to examine institutional practices that powerful people benefit from maintaining. A wellness webinar on stress management can be contracted, scheduled, and checked off a corporate social responsibility report in weeks. Restructuring workloads to be sustainable requires confronting why the workloads became unsustainable in the first place—which leads to questions about staffing decisions, budget priorities, and executive choices that the organization would prefer not to examine. Wellness programming is cheap. Workplace improvement is expensive. The industry exists because the cheap option also happens to be the one that leaves the power structure intact.

The EAP Failure

The Employee Assistance Program is supposed to be the safety net—the resource that catches people when workplace stress exceeds what wellness emails can address. My experience with the EAP revealed it as another form of the same structural limitation: a program that treats individual symptoms while leaving institutional causes untouched.

In Spring 2023, I called the EAP. My marriage was deteriorating. My workplace conditions were compounding the stress. I needed support—real support, not a phone number on a poster. The EAP counselor I spoke with dismissed my marriage concerns as a budgeting problem. Not a relationship crisis. Not a mental health issue. A budgeting problem. The implication was that if I managed my finances better, the marital stress would resolve itself. The actual situation—which was far more complex and would eventually involve discovering an affair—was reduced to a line item on a household budget.

I don't cite this to indict the individual counselor. EAP counselors operate under constraints—limited sessions, high volume, protocols designed for triage rather than deep therapeutic work. And to be fair, PPG's EAP did offer sessions, not just referrals. The structural problem is what happens when the initial interaction misses the mark and you need to find support elsewhere—when the standardized response doesn't fit the specific person.

Around this same period, a friend recommended Richard Reeves' *Of Boys and Men*, and the book reshaped how I thought about what I needed. Reeves documents the specific challenges men face in seeking mental health support—the socialization against vulnerability, the shortage of male therapists, the ways existing support structures often fail to meet men where they are. Reading it gave me language for something I'd been feeling but hadn't articulated: I didn't just need therapy. I needed to talk to someone who understood the specific pressures of navigating crisis as a man—the expectation to absorb, to perform stability, to manage without appearing to need management. I wanted to go with the outliers. To find the kind of support that matched the kind of struggle.

That choice—an informed, deliberate choice to seek a male therapist—is where the four-month wait came from. I called the EAP in April 2023. My marriage crisis was escalating—by mid-June, I would discover an affair that reframed everything I thought I knew about my personal life. But when I started searching for a male therapist, only two were available locally and accepting new patients. The shortage Reeves documents isn't abstract. It's a wait list. It's four months between recognizing what you need and accessing it. I didn't get into therapy until August 2023—four months during what was the most acute crisis period of my adult life.

Four months. In corporate terms, that's one quarterly business review. In human terms, it's the difference between catching a crisis and watching it metastasize. Between having someone in your corner when the worst happens and navigating the worst alone while waiting for a callback. The choice to seek the right support rather than the fastest support was the right one—August onward proved that. But the system shouldn't require that choice. The shortage of male mental health providers isn't a scheduling inconvenience. It's a structural gap that leaves men navigating their worst moments with fewer options than the wellness rhetoric acknowledges.

The corporate wellness model can point to the EAP and say "support was available." And it was—in the generic sense. What wasn't available was support that matched the specific needs of the specific person in the specific crisis. The gap between "we offer mental health resources" and "you can access the right mental health resource when you need it" is the gap between policy and practice that this entire series examines. The wellness infrastructure existed. The human infrastructure—enough male therapists, enough availability, enough nuance to match the right support to the right need—didn't.

Resilience as Individual Pathology

The word "resilience" has undergone a transformation in corporate language that strips it of its original meaning and weaponizes it against the people it's supposed to describe.

In psychology, resilience refers to the capacity to recover from adversity—a capacity that depends significantly on environmental factors like social support, material resources, and the absence of ongoing stressors. Resilience isn't a personality trait. It's an outcome that requires specific conditions. A person with strong support networks, adequate financial resources, and a stable environment is more likely to demonstrate resilience than a person without those things. The research is unambiguous: resilience is produced by conditions, not just by character.

In corporate language, resilience has become a character expectation. "We need resilient employees." "Our culture values resilience." "Building a resilient workforce." In this framing, resilience isn't an outcome of supportive conditions. It's a prerequisite for employment. The expectation isn't that the organization will create conditions where people can recover from difficulty. The expectation is that employees will absorb difficulty without requiring the organization to change anything.

This reframing accomplishes something specific: it transforms institutional failure into individual deficiency. When an employee burns out, the question becomes "why weren't they resilient enough?" rather than "what conditions produced the burnout?" When someone can't mask their neurodivergence through another round of open-plan office mandates, the diagnosis is "insufficient adaptability" rather than "inflexible environment." When the EAP wait time is four months and the employee's crisis doesn't wait, the organizational narrative frames the gap as a timing problem rather than a resource allocation failure.

The language performs a subtle inversion of cause and effect. The employee didn't burn out because the workload was unsustainable—they burned out because they lacked resilience. The team didn't disengage because the feedback culture was hostile—they disengaged because they lacked grit. The new hire didn't leave because the onboarding was inadequate—they left because they weren't a cultural fit. In each case, the institutional cause is erased and replaced with an individual explanation that requires no organizational change. The solution to every problem is a better employee, never a better workplace.

The resilience framework also provides cover for the conditions described throughout this series. Public shaming as feedback (Essay 10) becomes "honest culture that requires thick skin." Silence engineering (Essay 9) becomes "professional environment that values composure." Accommodation failure (Essay 12) becomes "high-performance culture that challenges everyone equally." The bureaucratic barriers of Essay 13 become "rigorous processes that ensure only genuine needs are addressed." Each instance of institutional dysfunction is reframed as an opportunity for individual growth—a test of character rather than a failure of systems. The resilience narrative doesn't just excuse the dysfunction. It valorizes it.

Resilience Versus Recovery

There's a distinction that corporate wellness rhetoric consistently collapses: the difference between resilience and recovery. They're not the same thing, and treating them as interchangeable causes real harm.

Resilience is bouncing back. It implies returning to a prior state—resuming normal function after disruption, restoring the status quo. In corporate contexts, resilience means returning to full productivity after adversity. The timeline is expected to be short. The disruption is expected to be contained. The person is expected to emerge from difficulty essentially unchanged, ready to resume their role as though the difficulty hadn't occurred.

Recovery is something different. Recovery is healing. It requires time, space, and conditions that may not resemble the prior state at all. Recovery acknowledges that some experiences change you—that you don't return to who you were before, but integrate the experience into a new understanding of yourself and your circumstances. Recovery doesn't have a corporate-friendly timeline. It doesn't optimize for productivity. It optimizes for the person.

Corporate culture demands resilience because resilience serves the organization. An employee who bounces back quickly is an employee whose disruption cost is minimized. Recovery, by contrast, might require time off, workload reduction, flexible arrangements, patience with reduced output, and the organizational humility to acknowledge that the workplace itself may have contributed to the harm being recovered from. Recovery costs the organization something. Resilience, in the corporate framing, costs only the employee.

The timelines reveal the value system. Bereavement leave: three to five days. EAP sessions: typically three to six. Short-term disability review: triggers after a defined absence period. Every organizational response to human crisis comes with an embedded timeline that communicates when the organization expects you to be "done"—when the disruption should be absorbed and the performance should resume. These timelines aren't based on recovery science. They're based on what the organization is willing to pay for. The gap between how long healing actually takes and how long the organization budgets for it is the gap between what the person needs and what the institution is willing to provide. And the resilience narrative fills that gap by reframing the person's unfinished recovery as a personal deficiency rather than an institutional shortfall.

When I was in crisis—Spring and Summer 2023, managing the personal and professional stressors I've described—what I needed was recovery. Time. Space. Support that acknowledged the complexity of what I was navigating. What the organization offered was the expectation of resilience: continue performing, continue masking, continue producing, and please avail yourself of the wellness resources we've provided. The resources were a meditation app and an EAP that dismissed my concerns and couldn't schedule therapy for four months. The expectation was unchanged performance. The gap between what I needed and what was offered was the gap between recovery and resilience—between what serves the person and what serves the institution.

What Came From Outside

The support that actually helped during and after the crisis didn't come from corporate programs. It came from community. A friend who listened without offering productivity advice. A mentor who'd navigated similar institutional betrayal and could validate the experience without minimizing it. An attorney who provided clarity in a system designed to produce confusion. The volunteer work I eventually found, where contribution was measured in human impact rather than quarterly metrics. The faith community that held space for genuine struggle without rushing toward resolution.

Each of these sources of support shared a common characteristic that the corporate wellness model structurally lacks: they were unconditional. The friend didn't listen because it reduced his healthcare costs. The mentor didn't validate my experience because it improved a retention metric. The volunteer organization didn't value my contribution because it served a business case. The support existed because the people providing it genuinely cared about the human being in front of them—not as a productivity unit, not as a liability to be managed, not as a wellness program participant whose utilization rate demonstrated ROI. As a person.

This isn't an argument against corporate wellness programs. It's an observation about their limits. The programs that helped were relational, not transactional. They were rooted in genuine human connection, not in benefit structures designed to manage organizational liability. They operated on the person's timeline, not the employer's. They required no documentation, no approval, no bureaucratic process. They were offered freely by people whose investment in my wellbeing wasn't contingent on my productivity.

The corporate wellness model can't replicate this because its fundamental orientation is different. Wellness programs exist to serve organizational outcomes—reduced healthcare costs, lower absenteeism, higher retention. When those outcomes align with employee wellbeing, the programs work. When they diverge—when the employee's genuine need is recovery time that reduces productivity, or accommodation that costs money, or the acknowledgment that the workplace itself is the stressor—the programs reveal their limits. They can offer a meditation app. They can't offer the structural change that would make the meditation unnecessary.

My Six Sigma Green Belt training—the process improvement methodology I learned at PPG—taught me to trace problems to their root causes rather than treating symptoms. Applied to corporate wellness, the diagnosis is straightforward: the root cause of employee burnout in dysfunctional workplaces is the dysfunction, not the employee's insufficient coping capacity. Treating burnout with resilience training is symptom management. Treating it with structural change is root cause analysis. The organization trained me to think this way, and then became an example of what happens when you don't.

The Strongest Thing

The resilience narrative carries a hidden assumption: that adaptation is always positive. That the ability to endure difficult conditions is a virtue. That the employee who stays, who masks, who copes, who meditates through the dysfunction and maintains output despite the conditions—that employee is the success story.

But sometimes the conditions shouldn't be endured. Sometimes the strongest thing you can do is refuse to adapt to what shouldn't be tolerated. Sometimes the failure of resilience—the moment when the mask breaks, when the performance becomes unsustainable, when the employee can't keep pretending that dysfunction is normal—is the most honest thing that happens in the organization. It's the canary in the coal mine doing what canaries do: signaling that the environment is toxic, not that the canary isn't tough enough.

My departure from PPG wasn't a resilience failure. It was a system operating as designed: identifying someone who had stopped pretending the conditions were acceptable, and removing them. The wellness emails continued after I left. The meditation apps were still available. The EAP still offered its limited sessions and its referrals and its four-month wait times. The conditions that had produced the burnout remained in place, ready to produce it again in the next person who wasn't resilient enough—which is to say, the next person honest enough to stop pretending.

Resilience shouldn't mean enduring dysfunction. It shouldn't mean absorbing institutional failure through individual effort. It shouldn't mean meditating your way through a feedback culture that shames rather than develops, or breathing through the anxiety of knowing that disclosure might trigger termination, or journaling your way through the gap between a company's stated values and its actual practices.

Resilience, in any meaningful sense, should mean the capacity to thrive—not in spite of the environment, but because of it. An organization that genuinely values resilience would build environments where resilience isn't constantly tested by institutional dysfunction. Where the coping skills taught in wellness programs supplement a healthy workplace rather than substitute for one. Where the word "resilience" describes what emerges from support, not what's demanded in its absence.

Until then, resilience will remain what it has become in corporate America: the expectation that you'll absorb the organization's failures without complaint, and the explanation, when you finally can't, for why the failure was yours.
