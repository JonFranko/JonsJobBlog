If It Wasn't Grown, It Isn't a Christmas Tree

By Jonathan Franko, November 25, 2026

If it wasn't grown, it isn't a Christmas tree. It's just a sparkling holiday decoration.

And the fake tree has company. These days the whole season comes out of a factory: the decor, the deals, even the start date. The tree just happens to be the one piece honest enough to call itself artificial.

An artificial Christmas tree is exactly what it says. An imitation. It didn't grow, it didn't take in a single season of rain, and nobody planted it. Somebody manufactured it so somebody else could sell it.

And the story of who manufactured it is even better than I expected when I set out to write this article.

A Toilet Brush With Ambition

You may have heard a toilet brush company invented the fake Christmas tree as we know it. That version is mostly myth, but the real story is better.

In the late 1950s, the American Brush Machinery Company in Mount Vernon, New York, had machines built to twist wire into brushes. The kind you scrub a toilet with. Business was bad, so they pointed those machines at Christmas. The first attempts flopped, and the company sent a machinist named Si Spiegel to shut the factory down. Instead, he brought in real pine trees to copy, improved the machines, and built the modern artificial tree business. By the mid-1970s his machines were turning out about 800,000 trees a year.

For the record, Spiegel was a genuine war hero who flew 35 missions in a B-17. No knock on his character, but by his granddaughter's account, he cared more about the machines he built than about the holiday itself. He solved the problem in front of him, and look at what the problem was. Nobody set out to improve Christmas. A factory needed something to make.

So that “tree” in your living room is, mechanically speaking, a toilet brush with ambition.

Plastic wasn't the first fake, either. In the 1880s, Germans started making trees out of green-dyed goose feathers wrapped around wire branches. In fairness, those had a decent excuse: people were worried about cutting down too many evergreens for the holiday. That problem got solved the right way. Today most real Christmas trees are grown on farms as a crop and replanted, not cut from wild forests. The excuse expired. The replicas stayed.

Before plastic took over, there was aluminum. The Evergleam rolled out of Manitowoc, Wisconsin, in 1959, and you couldn't string lights on it without risking a shock, so families aimed a spinning color wheel at it instead. Then in 1965, A Charlie Brown Christmas aired, a whole special about Christmas getting too commercial. Lucy sends Charlie Brown out for a big shiny aluminum tree, and he comes back with a scrawny real one instead. He was right then, and he would still be right if the special premiered today.

The Season That Keeps Getting Longer

None of this happened in a vacuum. There's a name for the holiday showing up earlier every year: Christmas creep. Merriam-Webster notes the phrase never gets used as a compliment. It's shorthand for "existential dread and disgust." Sounds about right.

The complaint is old. A Washington newspaper was grumbling back in 1883 that the holiday trade season seemed to start earlier every year. But the best example is 1939.

That year Thanksgiving fell on November 30, the latest possible date, and back then stores didn't push Christmas before Thanksgiving. Retailers wanted more shopping days. A retail trade group made its case to the Commerce Department, and FDR moved Thanksgiving up a week. Critics called it Franksgiving. The states split almost down the middle, so for a few years the country couldn't agree on when Thanksgiving was. Congress finally locked in the fourth Thursday at the end of 1941, and the federal government came to a consensus to move a national holiday so stores could sell more stuff.

Today nobody needs the President's help. Costco and Home Depot put holiday decor out in August. Amazon, Walmart, and Target run "holiday kickoff" sales the first week of October, before most kids have picked a Halloween costume.

Here's the part the analyst in me likes. The National Retail Federation's own 2026 survey found 43 percent of shoppers plan to start before November. Last year it was 42. The starting line keeps moving, and shoppers barely move with it. That isn't demand. That's a push. The season isn't getting longer because people want it longer. It's getting longer because there's more stuff to sell, most of which nobody needs.

The fake tree and the fake season are the same product. Both are built to look like the real thing without the wait. A real tree takes most of a decade of rain and sun. The real holiday feeling takes its own season: the first snow, Thanksgiving dinner, the week the neighbors finally hang their lights. Neither can be rushed, so the industry built substitutes. A tree that's ready whenever the shipment lands, and a season that starts whenever the inventory does.

Nobody wakes up in August wanting a seven-foot lighted lamp post, but that's when Costco put one out. That want has to be manufactured the same way the needles are. Real demand grows. Artificial demand gets installed.

Speaking of things with "artificial" in the name, my LinkedIn feed this year is full of posts announcing that AI makes human judgment more important than ever. I use AI every day, and I agree. I'd just add that judgment grows the same way a tree does.

What Makes a Tree

Before I spent my days in spreadsheets, I was a forester. So let me defend the real thing on its own terms.

Start with the wind. A tree that gets pushed around by it ends up shorter and stouter, with a thicker trunk and more of its effort sunk into roots. Foresters have noticed this for more than two centuries. Stake a sapling too tight for too long and it never gets to sway, so it never toughens up, and it's more likely to snap or tip over once the stakes come off. Being sheltered isn't the favor it looks like.

A Christmas tree farm is a slow business. Penn State Extension puts it at eight to ten years to grow one. For all of those years, that tree is pulling carbon out of the air and holding soil in place. The rows between the trees fill in with ground covers like clover and wildflowers. One study counted 80 plant species in tree farm ground covers, milkweed included, drawing bees and the insects that eat crop pests. Andy Finton, a forest ecologist with The Nature Conservancy in Massachusetts, has said tree farms can work a lot like young forests. When the Society for the Protection of New Hampshire Forests started one, bobolinks and woodcock nested between the young rows, deer hid their fawns in the grass, and kestrels and harriers showed up to hunt the mice. And when a tree gets cut, growers typically plant one to three seedlings to replace it.

Yes, a cut tree eventually gives most of its carbon back. But the farm keeps those acres growing year after year. That's a cycle. It keeps land in trees instead of in another strip mall.

"But Real Trees Get Sold Too"

They do. The difference is where the money goes and what's left behind.

Pennsylvania had 1,301 Christmas tree farms in the 2022 USDA Census of Agriculture. Growers here cut more than 720,000 trees that year, fifth in the country. Buy one of those, and the money goes to a grower down the road who puts another seedling in the ground.

Buy the box, and a slice goes to a factory in China, where nearly all of them are made. The rest goes to an importer and a big-box store's fourth quarter.

Then there's the trip. A container ship from Shanghai to Los Angeles crosses about 6,500 miles of open Pacific, and it's roughly another 2,400 by rail or truck to Pittsburgh. Call it 9,000 miles for a tree that never grew. A tree from a Pennsylvania farm might travel thirty.

To be fair, the miles aren't the worst part. Ocean freight is efficient per box. The UK's Carbon Trust puts a 6.5-foot artificial tree at about 88 pounds of greenhouse gas emissions, and roughly two-thirds of that is the plastic itself, which starts as oil. Another quarter comes from the factory. Making the thing is the problem, more than moving it.

What you get for all that is mostly metal and PVC, a petroleum-based plastic that's among the hardest to recycle. When you're done with it, it goes to a landfill and stays there. A real tree goes to the curb in January and comes back as mulch, and chipped like that, the Carbon Trust puts its footprint under 8 pounds.

The Honest Carbon Math

I won't pretend this one is a blowout. The American Christmas Tree Association, the trade group for artificial tree makers, paid for a study that put the break-even point at 4.7 years. Less biased reviews land somewhere between five and ten. Bert Cregg, a horticulture and forestry professor at Michigan State, has said the industry study's parameters were too narrow. His bottom line is that both options have a small environmental footprint, and the winner mostly depends on how long you keep the fake one and how far you drive for the real one.

So if you already own a fake tree, keep using it. Hauling it to a landfill next week only makes it worse.

But the case for a real tree was never about a spreadsheet, and I say that as someone who has built plenty of them.

Why the Distinction Is Important

It's worth remembering what the tree was supposed to mean. Long before anyone sold one, people brought evergreens inside in the dead of winter because they stayed green when everything else looked dead. They were a promise that life makes it through the cold. The whole symbol is a living thing that refuses to die in December. A plastic tree can't carry that. It's a symbol of life that was never alive.

A real tree drops needles. It needs water. It makes the house smell like the woods for three weeks and leaves you vacuuming in February. That's not a flaw. That's the point. It's alive, it came from somewhere, and somebody grew it.

Cut a real tree open and you can read its life. Wide rings for the easy years, narrow ones for drought, scars where fire came through and it kept growing anyway. Judgment is the same kind of record. It's built from the calls you got wrong and had to live with, and there's no way to install it.

Celebrate It After, Not Before

So how do you push back? Stop starting Christmas before Thanksgiving, and keep it going after the 25th instead.

Fair warning: do this and somebody will call you a Grinch. Which is a little ridiculous. Nobody calls you unpatriotic for not loving a late-night fireworks show in your neighborhood several months before Independence Day. And anyone who remembers the story knows the Grinch took every present, tree, and stocking in town, and Christmas showed up anyway. That's the whole moral, and it's on tradition’s side.

The other name you'll hear is Scrooge, which gets it even more backwards. Scrooge didn't hate Christmas for showing up too early. He hated what it cost him, calling it "a poor excuse for picking a man's pocket every twenty-fifth of December." The one treating Christmas like a line item isn't the person waiting until after Thanksgiving.

That isn't a new idea. It's the old one. In church tradition, the Christmas season starts on December 25. The Twelve Days of Christmas run from Christmas Day to Epiphany on January 6, and in some older traditions the greenery stayed up until Candlemas on February 2.

The holidays used to run on the church calendar and the seasons. Now they run on the fiscal calendar. Many big retailers close their books at the end of January, so the whole holiday season lands in their final quarter, and every extra week of "holiday shopping" pads Q4. Christmas creep isn't about Christmas. It's about year-end numbers.

So go by the traditional calendar. October belongs to Halloween and November belongs to Thanksgiving. January and February are the dead of winter, the days are short, and whatever you still want for the holidays is on clearance anyway. They're also when Orthodox Christmas and Lunar New Year arrive, after Hanukkah and Kwanzaa light up December. People have always found reasons to celebrate in the dark months. If you really need three months of Christmas, take them from the back end, where you'll be in good company.

Dickens had it right. By the end of the book, nobody praised Scrooge for starting Christmas early. They said he knew how to keep Christmas well. Keeping it is the part we've lost.

The tree might not make it to March, and that's fine. Bring it home in mid-December, keep it through the Twelve Days, and let the lights carry the rest of winter. Real things have a season.

If it wasn't grown, it isn't a tree. It's just a sparkling holiday decoration.

Nothing wrong with decorations. Just don't mistake a counterfeit for the real thing. Same goes for anything else with "artificial" in the name this year.
