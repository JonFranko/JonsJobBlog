The Bureaucracy of Disbelief

Part II: The Human Cost Feb 19, 2026 · By Jonathan Franko
Series Note: This essay examines the systems that are supposed to catch what the previous essays describe—the HR processes, complaint mechanisms, and legal remedies that exist, in theory, to protect employees. The question isn't whether they exist. It's who they're designed to protect.

My name was misspelled in the severance agreement. It was misspelled in the company's legal response to my complaint. It was misspelled in HR correspondence generated after four and a half years of employment. Small errors. The kind that get dismissed as carelessness—someone typing too quickly, a system auto-filling the wrong field. But when the organization handling your separation, your legal proceedings, and your employment records can't spell your name correctly after nearly five years, the errors stop looking like accidents and start looking like a message: you were processed, not considered.

This essay is about the processing. About the systems that exist between an employee's experience of harm and any possibility of accountability—the HR departments, the complaint processes, the legal mechanisms that create the appearance of recourse while structurally disadvantaging the person seeking it. It's about what happens when you try to use the systems that organizations point to as evidence of fairness, and discover that the systems are designed to produce resolution for the institution, not justice for the individual.

HR as Institutional Defense

The most important thing I learned about HR is something every employee eventually learns but few say out loud: HR exists to protect the organization from legal liability, not to advocate for employees. This isn't a cynical interpretation. It's a structural reality. HR reports to leadership. HR's budget comes from the company. HR's performance is measured by the organization's exposure to risk—legal risk, regulatory risk, reputational risk. When an employee's interests align with the organization's risk profile, HR appears helpful. When those interests diverge, HR's structural allegiance becomes unmistakable.

I saw this most clearly in the accommodation process. The "interactive process" for disability accommodations—the term of art from the ADA—is designed to appear open, collaborative, and responsive. In practice, it creates a specific set of barriers that systematically advantage the institution. The employee must disclose their disability. They must provide documentation. They must propose accommodations. They must demonstrate that the accommodations are "reasonable." Each step requires the employee to make themselves more visible, more documented, and more vulnerable—while the organization maintains the ability to evaluate, delay, modify, or deny the request at each stage.

Consider the asymmetry embedded in each step. The employee discloses—revealing medical information in a professional context where that information carries career risk. The organization evaluates—using criteria it defines, on a timeline it controls, through a process it administers. The employee proposes—articulating what they need in language the organization will accept, which often requires legal or medical expertise the employee doesn't have. The organization decides—weighing the accommodation against business interests that it defines and the employee can't verify. At every stage, the employee gives something (privacy, time, vulnerability) and the organization retains something (control, information, discretion). The "interactive" process is interactive in the same way that a negotiation between a person who needs water and a person who controls the well is interactive. Both parties participate. Only one party has leverage.

The paradox is structural: safety requires confidentiality, but accommodation requires visibility. You can't get accommodated for a condition you haven't disclosed. But disclosing the condition creates a record that the organization can use in future employment decisions—or, more precisely, that the organization can claim it didn't use, because the information was "siloed" from decision-makers through the same HR systems that collected it.

This is plausible deniability engineered through organizational design. The accommodation request goes to HR. HR maintains confidentiality. Decision-makers claim they didn't know. The system that collected the information about your disability is the same system that ensures the organization can deny awareness of it when the timing of your termination raises questions. The bureaucracy doesn't gaslight on purpose. It gaslights by design.

The Documentation Burden

Filing a complaint—whether internal or external—requires documentation. Dates, times, specific statements, witnesses, patterns. The documentation burden falls entirely on the complainant, who is simultaneously managing the emotional and financial consequences of whatever happened, while the institution has legal counsel on retainer, access to all internal records, and the organizational resources to construct a comprehensive defense.

The asymmetry is staggering. I spent months assembling documentation for my complaint—reviewing emails, reconstructing timelines, cross-referencing dates, trying to build from personal records and memory the kind of evidentiary narrative that the company could assemble in hours from its own systems. The company had access to every email I'd sent, every meeting I'd attended, every HR record, every performance review, every system log. I had what I'd saved, what I remembered, and what I could reconstruct from available evidence.

This information asymmetry isn't incidental to the complaint process. It's foundational. Proving a pattern of discrimination requires demonstrating intent—showing that the adverse action was connected to the protected characteristic rather than to legitimate business reasons. But proving intent requires access to internal communications, decision-making records, and organizational data that the complainant doesn't have and the institution controls. You have to prove what people were thinking when they made decisions, using evidence that the people who made those decisions are not required to share.

The documentation burden also creates a class barrier that mirrors the one I described in Essay 11's analysis of severance agreements. Assembling a complaint requires time, literacy in legal and bureaucratic language, access to healthcare documentation for disability claims, and the emotional capacity to revisit traumatic experiences in the clinical, evidentiary format that the process demands. People with resources—savings, family support, professional connections—can absorb these costs. People without them are effectively priced out of the process that's supposed to protect them.

I had an MBA and enough analytical training to assemble a structured complaint. I had enough savings—barely—to sustain myself through the process. I had the cognitive capacity, even while managing ADHD and the aftermath of termination, to navigate bureaucratic language and legal requirements. And it was still overwhelming. The amount of time, energy, and emotional labor required to document what happened—to transform lived experience into evidentiary format—consumed months that could have been spent recovering, job searching, or rebuilding. If the process was this draining for someone with my educational and analytical background, the barrier for someone without those resources is effectively prohibitive.

The extensive documentation required for accommodations assumes bad faith on the part of the person requesting them. You must prove your disability is "real"—as though someone would voluntarily navigate this labyrinth for accommodations they don't need. You must demonstrate the accommodation is "reasonable"—a standard defined by the institution evaluating the request, not by the person whose ability to work depends on it. Meanwhile, the same organization that required medical documentation to justify flexible work arrangements needed no documentation whatsoever to invite me to speak at a mental health awareness series. Visibility for PR purposes was effortless. Accommodation for actual work required a bureaucratic process designed to make you prove you deserved it.

The contrast between what the organization required to use my experience for its benefit (nothing—just an invitation) and what it required to accommodate my condition for mine (disclosure, documentation, medical verification, managerial approval) encapsulates the entire paradox of corporate disability inclusion. Your story is a resource to be extracted at the organization's convenience. Your needs are a liability to be evaluated at the organization's discretion.

The Application Process as Preview

Before the complaint process, there was the application process—and the patterns were the same.

When I applied for an internal position in February 2024, the application included disability self-identification. This information, according to company policy, goes to EEO tracking systems and is siloed from hiring managers. The purpose of the self-identification is to allow the company to monitor its own diversity metrics—to demonstrate, on surveys like the Disability Equality Index, that it collects the data and tracks the outcomes.

The siloing creates the same plausible deniability that the accommodation process creates. The company collects the information. The company maintains it in a separate system. Decision-makers claim they didn't have access. The structural design of the information system becomes the legal defense: we couldn't have discriminated based on disability because we didn't know about the disability, because our systems are designed to prevent that knowledge from reaching decision-makers.

Whether this design actually prevents information from flowing is a question the design itself makes unanswerable from the outside. The company points to the structural separation as proof that no improper information transfer occurred. The employee points to the timeline—disclosure in February, termination in April—and asks how the timing can be coincidental. Neither can prove the other wrong, because the system is designed to operate in exactly the ambiguity that makes proof impossible.

After termination, the application system produced its own form of bureaucratic messaging. Reapplication attempts were flagged as incomplete despite meeting every visible submission requirement. The system—whether through design or coincidence—created a loop: technically welcome to reapply, practically unable to complete the process. Each "incomplete" notification communicated something the company would never say directly: you are not wanted here. But the message came through a system, not a person, which meant there was no one to challenge, no decision to appeal, and no documentation of intent.

Automated systems create plausible deniability at scale. When a person blocks you, there's a decision to examine. When a system blocks you, there's only a technical issue to troubleshoot. The distinction between institutional intent and system error becomes impossible to establish—which is, from the institution's perspective, exactly the point.

The Commission and Its Limits

When internal systems fail—when HR protects the institution, when the accommodation process creates barriers, when the application system produces mysterious errors—the external remedy is supposed to be the complaint process. For me, that meant the Pittsburgh Commission on Human Relations, case EO-2025-044.

I filed the complaint because the internal systems had failed and because the timeline between disclosure and termination raised questions that deserved answers. I filed it understanding that the process would be long, adversarial, and structured in ways that favored the institution. What I didn't fully understand until I was inside the process was how thoroughly the external system reproduces the same asymmetries as the internal one.

The company's legal response was instructive. Immediate denial. No self-examination. No acknowledgment that the timeline raised legitimate questions. No engagement with the substance of the complaint. Just categorical rejection—the institutionalized narcissism I've observed throughout this series, where confrontation with evidence produces denial rather than reflection. The response was prepared by attorneys whose professional obligation is to protect the company, using the company's records, on the company's timeline. My response was prepared by me, from my records, on whatever timeline the commission's process allowed.

The name misspellings continued in the legal documents. The same carelessness—or indifference—that characterized my employment characterized the legal proceedings that followed it. After four and a half years and a formal legal complaint, the institution still couldn't spell my name. The symbolism was too precise to be unintentional: even in the process of defending itself against a claim of institutional disregard, the institution demonstrated exactly the disregard being alleged.

The EEOC and state commission processes create the appearance of accountability without, in many cases, the substance. Complaints are filed, investigated, and resolved—but the investigation is limited by the commission's resources, which are a fraction of the company's legal budget. The burden of proof remains on the complainant. The company controls the information flow. The process takes months or years, during which the complainant is unemployed, the evidence ages, and the emotional cost of reliving the experience accumulates without any guarantee of resolution.

The emotional cost deserves specific attention because it's absent from every formal analysis of the complaint process. Each stage of the process requires the complainant to revisit the experience in detail—to recall specific dates, specific statements, specific moments of humiliation or harm, and to present them in the clinical, evidentiary format that the process demands. This isn't remembering. It's re-experiencing, in a context that treats your worst professional moments as material to be evaluated for legal sufficiency. The process doesn't ask "what happened to you?" It asks "can you prove what happened to you to a standard that would survive adversarial cross-examination?" The distinction between those questions is the distance between support and litigation.

And the severance agreement complicates everything. As I described in Essay 11, the comprehensive release of claims I signed under financial pressure limited my legal options before the complaint process even began. The system is sequential in a way that advantages the institution at every stage: terminate, present severance under duress, purchase the release of claims, and then respond to any remaining complaint with the defense that the claims have already been released. Each stage narrows the employee's options while preserving the institution's.

Institutional Betrayal

Psychologist Jennifer Freyd developed the concept of institutional betrayal to describe what happens when an institution that is supposed to protect a person instead causes or fails to prevent harm. The concept was originally applied to universities and military organizations, but it describes the corporate complaint process with precision.

The betrayal isn't the initial harm—the termination, the discrimination, the failure to accommodate. The betrayal is what happens after. It's the complaint system that requires you to prove you deserve help while the institution uses the same system to defend itself. It's the HR process that appears responsive while structurally protecting the organization's interests over yours. It's the legal response that denies everything categorically without engaging with the substance of what happened. It's the discovery that every system between you and accountability was designed by and for the institution you're trying to hold accountable.

Freyd's research shows that institutional betrayal compounds the psychological damage of the original harm. The harm itself is traumatic. The discovery that the systems meant to address the harm are actually extensions of it—that the complaint process re-traumatizes rather than resolves—creates a secondary injury that can be more damaging than the first. You don't just lose trust in the specific institution. You lose trust in institutional remedies as a concept. You learn that the systems designed to create fairness are themselves unfair, and that knowledge follows you into every subsequent institutional interaction.

The compounding effect is what distinguishes institutional betrayal from simple institutional failure. A system that tries to help and fails is disappointing. A system that claims to help while structurally protecting the entity you're complaining about is betrayal. The difference matters psychologically because betrayal by a trusted system undermines the cognitive framework that makes seeking help possible. If the system designed to address harm is itself harmful, then seeking help becomes another risk to calculate rather than a remedy to pursue. The lesson teaches itself: you're on your own.

I carry this now. Not as bitterness—I've been clear throughout this series that bitterness isn't the register I'm writing in. But as knowledge. Knowledge that HR is a defense mechanism. Knowledge that complaint processes favor institutions. Knowledge that automated systems create ambiguity that institutions exploit. Knowledge that the documentation burden falls on the person with the fewest resources. This knowledge is useful. It makes me a more informed participant in every future workplace interaction. But it shouldn't be necessary. The systems should work. And the fact that learning how they actually work requires being harmed by them is itself an indictment.

Institutional trust isn't binary. It can be rebuilt—but only in organizations that demonstrate through repeated action that their systems do what they claim. Not perfectly. Not every time. But consistently enough that navigating them doesn't feel like another form of institutional gaslighting. I've found that since leaving PPG, and I'll explore what rebuilding looks like in Part III. But the contrast between systems designed to process people and systems designed to serve them is sharp enough to confirm that the problem wasn't my expectations. It was the design.

The Design, Not the Accident

Every element of the bureaucratic system I've described—the documentation burden, the information asymmetry, the plausible deniability, the sequential narrowing of options, the name misspellings that signal institutional indifference—could be explained individually as an imperfection. Systems are imperfect. People make mistakes. Bureaucracies are slow. These explanations are available for each element in isolation.

But when every imperfection advantages the same party—when every "mistake" makes it harder for the employee and easier for the institution—the pattern stops looking accidental and starts looking architectural. The system isn't broken. It's working exactly as designed. It's designed to process complaints, not resolve them. To create the appearance of accountability, not the practice of it. To protect the institution from liability while exposing the complainant to exhaustion, financial pressure, and the re-traumatization of reliving the harm through an adversarial process.

The bureaucracy of disbelief doesn't announce itself. It's polite. It's procedural. It asks you to fill out forms, attend meetings, provide documentation. It creates the appearance of process while ensuring that the process itself is the obstacle. And by the time you realize the system isn't designed to help you—that it's designed to outlast you—you're too exhausted to fight it.

That's not a bug. That's the design. And understanding it is the first step toward building something different—systems that measure outcomes rather than inputs, that distribute the documentation burden equitably, that treat the complainant's experience as evidence rather than allegation, and that hold institutions accountable for the gap between the processes they advertise and the results those processes produce.

What would equitable systems look like? They would start by acknowledging the power asymmetry instead of pretending it doesn't exist. They would provide complainants with access to the same institutional records that the company uses to construct its defense—not unlimited access, but enough to evaluate their own claims on an informed basis. They would fund independent advocacy for complainants through the same budget that funds the company's legal defense—if the process is genuinely neutral, independent advocacy shouldn't threaten the outcome. They would measure the complaint process not by how many complaints were processed but by how many complainants reported that the process was fair, responsive, and conducted in good faith.

These aren't radical proposals. They're the minimum requirements for a system that does what it claims to do. The fact that they would represent fundamental changes to current practice tells you how far current practice has drifted from its stated purpose.

Until then, the bureaucracy will continue to do what bureaucracies do: process people with the same efficiency and indifference with which it misspells their names.
